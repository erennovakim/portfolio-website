Cosi: Taking the Awkwardness Out of Living Together

A co-living app that handles the conversations roommates avoid, so chores get done and friendships survive.

Roommate problems rarely start with a mess. They start with silence.

Roughly one in three college students has conflict with a roommate. When we dug into why, the cause wasn't usually the dishes or the noise. It was that telling someone you live with that something bothers you is uncomfortable. So most people say nothing, clean up quietly, and let resentment build.

Every shared house we heard about broke down in the same three ways:

Uneven responsibility

Chores quietly fall on whoever cares most.

Confrontation awkwardness

People avoid bringing up cleaning, noise, or guests, in person and over text.

Failed informal systems

Fridge lists, whiteboards, and group chats start strong and fizzle out, because nothing keeps them going.

How might we encourage people to be better roommates by opening channels of neutral communication, visualizing responsibilities, and emphasizing individual impact?

Half of the students we surveyed find it hard to confront a roommate, even when something is clearly wrong.

To check that this wasn't just our own experience, I wrote and ran a survey with 48 college students and asked an open-ended question about their most common roommate issues. I coded the responses into themes:

  • Cleaning came up in 16 responses, a third of everyone surveyed, without being prompted
  • Noise: 10
  • Guests: 9
  • Hygiene: 3

Two more numbers shaped the project. 50% said it's difficult to confront their roommates and communicate ongoing issues. 67% said a shared roommate calendar for scheduling cleaning and guests would be useful (31% said no, 2% maybe).

The problem wasn't that students didn't know what bothered them. It was that they had no comfortable way to say it.

50% find it hard to confront a roommate

67% want a shared calendar for cleaning and guests

In interviews, two words kept coming up: "nobody" and "never."

I interviewed students about how their households actually manage chores and communication. Two patterns showed up again and again.

Lack of accountability

"Sent out a schedule but only 2/4 roommates actually cleaned."

"We have a standing to-do list on the fridge, no one seems to do things though."

Hard to communicate

"There is no communication happening."

"I would say never verbally and through text in terms of chores."

"Everyone is so dumb, I'm just trying to wait it out."

That last quote stuck with me. When people would rather wait out a lease than have one conversation, the problem isn't chores. It's the conversation.

The research boiled down to two needs: accountability without nagging, and communication without confrontation.

Putting the survey and interviews side by side, every pain point fit under one of two needs. These became the design principles we checked every feature against.

Accountability without nagging

The system should remind people, so roommates don't have to.

Communication without confrontation

Hard conversations should have a neutral starting point.

Existing apps track bills, chores, or calendars, but none of them help roommates talk to each other.

We audited four tools people already use to manage shared living. Every one of them treats co-living as a logistics problem. None of them address the part our research said hurts most: the conversation. That's the gap Cosi was designed to fill.

I mapped the flows around one goal: get roommates aligned once, so they don't have to keep negotiating.

Before designing screens, I mapped how a user moves through Cosi: setting up a household, creating a Roommate Agreement together, then getting into a daily loop of seeing tasks, completing them, and picking up unassigned messes.

The key decision was putting the Roommate Agreement up front. If expectations are set together at the start, every later reminder enforces something everyone already agreed to, instead of one roommate's opinion.

From the flows, I sketched lo-fi screens and built mid-fi wireframes for the core screens before moving to visual design.

Our first design worked on paper, but users told us it felt like the wrong app.

Our first visual direction used a soft pink palette. Before going further, I gathered open-ended feedback from 10 target users on the branding, colors, fonts, and how the design made them feel.

The feedback was clear: it looked too much like Flo, a period-tracking app. Instead of feeling warm and safe, it felt unfamiliar for what the app was meant to do.

Next, I ran usability testing with 4 target users on the core flows. Two more problems came up:

  • The "Claim" button was hard to read. Low-contrast red on pink made one participant visibly strain to read it, and this was the main action on the Unassigned Tasks screen.
  • People couldn't find their cleaning days. On a plain calendar, users weren't sure where to see when it was their turn.

Taken together, the feedback said the same thing: the interface was adding stress to an experience that was supposed to remove it.

So we redesigned around one feeling: coming home to a warm, lamp-lit room.

Rather than patching the issues one at a time, we treated them as a single brief. The new direction took inspiration from cozy interior lighting: warm oranges, deep browns, and calm greens, with Poppins for a friendly, rounded type voice.

Meet Bob the Blob

Bob is our team's mascot and the voice of the app. Bob delivers reminders, reacts to your status in the app, and suggests conversation starters. That matters because a reminder from Bob doesn't feel like a roommate nagging you. Bob turns "you didn't do the dishes" into a neutral nudge no one has to take personally.

Every feature answers one of the two needs from research.

Roommate Agreement: a neutral place to start the hard conversation

Designed solo

Why an agreement? Interviewees had schedules, but nobody had agreed to them, so nobody followed them. The agreement makes expectations shared from day one.

Housemates set the cleaning schedule, rotation order, checklist, policies, and shared items together. Bob suggests prompts like "How often should we clean?" and "How long can items sit out?" so nobody has to be the one who brings it up. Once it's saved, Cosi rotates the tasks automatically.

I designed this flow end to end. The biggest challenge was length: an agreement covers a lot, and onboarding can't feel like paperwork. I grouped it into clear sections (schedule, checklist, policies, shared items) and added a save-draft option so housemates don't have to finish in one sitting.

My Tasks and Unassigned Tasks: handling messes without pointing fingers

Co-designed

Why an unassigned pool? Not every mess fits a schedule. Food left out on the counter used to mean a passive-aggressive text. Now it's a task anyone can see.

My Tasks shows your assigned chores for the week. Unassigned Tasks is a shared pool where anyone can post a quick task, and anyone can Claim it or mark it Resolved. No names, no blame, just something that needs doing.

Home: your impact, at a glance

Co-designed

Why a dashboard? Our persona's frustration was feeling like her effort went unseen. The home screen makes everyone's contribution visible.

Home shows today's tasks, upcoming events, and a short report on how consistently you've been completing tasks, so individual impact is concrete instead of assumed.

The Thank You button: making appreciation as easy as a reminder

My screen

Why a thank-you? Accountability alone can feel like surveillance. We wanted positive communication to be just as easy as reminders.

One tap sends a housemate a notification: "Sam thanked you! Thank you for helping out with everything recently!" Roommates feel appreciated, and seeing others get shoutouts motivates everyone to pitch in.

Calendar: your turn, instantly visible

Co-designed

Scheduled cleaning days get wave and bubble markers, so you can see whose turn it is at a glance. This came directly from usability testing.

After the redesign, users finished tasks faster and were far less confused.

In follow-up user testing with the redesigned app, participants completed tasks more quickly and were less confused about how the app works than in the first round.

The changes that looked cosmetic, like color, contrast, and a few calendar graphics, turned out to be functional. Making Cosi feel calmer also made it easier to use.

The hardest part wasn't designing the app. It was remembering why we were building it.

Midway through the quarter, we got caught up in brainstorming features and lost track of how each one answered our problem statement. What brought us back was the research: Hannah's quote, the fridge to-do list nobody touched, the student waiting out the lease. We started checking every feature against the two needs: accountability and communication.

What I'm taking with me

  • When you lose the thread, go back to the research. It's the fastest way to know what matters and what's just scope creep.
  • Big changes late in a project are worth it. Rebranding after the A/B feedback felt risky, but it improved both how the app felt and how well it worked.
  • The process isn't a straight line. We went back to testing, redesigned, and tested again. Being flexible made the product stronger.

Cosi started as a chore app. By the end, I understood it as something closer to a translator: a way to say the hard thing without anyone having to be the bad guy. That's the kind of design I want to keep doing, where the interface takes on the discomfort so people don't have to.
